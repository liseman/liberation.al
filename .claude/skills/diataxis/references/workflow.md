# Diátaxis as a guide to work

Diátaxis is not only a guide to content. It is also a guide to process, and its process
advice runs counter to much accepted wisdom: **it discourages planning and top-down
workflows, preferring small, responsive iterations from which overall patterns emerge.**

## A guide, not a plan

The structure Diátaxis proposes is not a plan you must complete. It is a guide, a map that
helps you check you are in the right place and heading in the right direction. Its point
is to give you a way to think about your documentation so you can see what it is doing,
identify where the problems lie, and judge what would improve it.

## Do not worry about structure

Structure is key to documentation, and yet using Diátaxis means **not spending energy
trying to get the structure right**.

If you keep following the prompts Diátaxis provides, the documentation will eventually
assume the Diátaxis structure. It will have assumed that shape *because it was improved*.
It is not the other way round. Structure is not imposed to produce improvement.

Getting started does not require dividing the docs into four sections. It certainly does
not mean creating empty tutorial/how-to/reference/explanation folders with nothing in
them. **Do not do that.** Make changes where you see opportunities for improvement, and at
some point the changes will appear to demand that material move under a certain heading.
That is how the top-level structure forms: from the inside.

## Work one step at a time

Whatever state the documentation is in, even a complete mess by any standard, it can be
improved iteratively.

The urge to complete a large tranche of work before publishing, so there is something
substantial to show, should be resisted. **Every step in the right direction is worth
publishing immediately.**

Diátaxis provides a big picture, but do not work on the big picture. It is both
unnecessary and unhelpful. It is designed to guide small steps.

## Just do something

When facing a large mess, the temptation is to tear it down and start again. Avoid it. It
is not even necessary to go looking for things to improve.

1. **Choose something.** Any piece of the documentation. If you do not already know what
   you want to put right, do not go hunting for problems. Look at what is in front of you:
   the file you are in, the last page you read. If there is nothing, choose at random.
2. **Assess it.** Preferably something small, no bigger than a page, better still a
   paragraph or a sentence. Challenge it against the standards: *What user need does this
   represent? How well does it serve that need? What can be added, moved, removed or
   changed to serve it better? Do its language and logic meet the requirements of this
   mode of documentation?*
3. **Decide what to do.** *What single next action will produce an immediate improvement
   here?*
4. **Do it.** Complete that action and consider it completed: publish it, or at least
   commit it. You do not need to do anything else for it to be a worthy improvement.

Then go back to the beginning.

Working this way removes the most paralysing part of documentation work: deciding what to
do. It keeps work flowing towards the desired end without spending energy on a plan.

## Organic growth

A good model for documentation is well-formed organic growth that adapts to external
conditions. Growth happens at the cellular level. The structure of the organism is
guaranteed by the healthy development of its cells, each according to rules appropriate to
its kind. Structure is not imposed from above or outside; good structure develops from
within.

The same holds for documentation. Follow the principles for each piece, and the whole
attains a healthy structure because its components are well-formed, built from the
inside out, one cell at a time.

## Complete, not finished

A plant is never *finished*: it can always develop further. But at every stage, from seed
to mature tree, it is always *complete*. Nothing is missing from it. It is in a state
appropriate to its stage of development.

Documentation is the same. It is never finished, because it must keep adapting to the
product and to users' needs. But it can always be complete: useful, appropriate to its
current stage, structurally healthy, and ready for the next step.

## Applying this as an agent

Specific implications when doing documentation work on someone's behalf:

- **Ship the small thing.** When asked to improve docs, make one real improvement and show
  it. Do not return a plan when a change was possible.
- **Do not volunteer a migration.** If asked to fix a page, fix that page. Note the
  systemic issue in one sentence; do not restructure the tree uninvited.
- **Never scaffold.** Empty folders and stub pages with "TODO" are worse than nothing.
  They advertise absence and they rot.
- **Prefer deleting to writing.** Removing material that serves no need is often the
  highest-value single action available.
- **Publishing is part of the step.** An improvement that is not committed is not
  complete.
- **Leave it complete.** Whatever state you stop in should be coherent and useful, not
  half-migrated.
